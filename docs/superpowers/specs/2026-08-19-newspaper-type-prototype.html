Andy Burnham takes office, and rewires the state

Britain changed Prime Minister while the House of Commons was in recess. The machinery of government was redrawn by written statement, the elected House unable to ask a single question of it; the only question put came from a peer.

Britain changed Prime Minister with its elected House on holiday. The Commons rose on Thursday 16 July for a recess that runs to 31 August; Sir Keir Starmer had taken his last Prime Minister's Questions the day before, and the Speaker announced it from the Chair as "the final Prime Minister's questions with the right hon. and learned Member for Holborn and St Pancras (Keir Starmer) as Prime Minister." On Monday 20 July Andy Burnham became Prime Minister. On Tuesday 21 July a written statement, laid in the House of Lords in the name of the Lord Privy Seal and made on the Prime Minister's behalf, informed Parliament that "Today, we are fundamentally rewiring the state, changing the geography of government." Only one House was sitting to be told, and it was the unelected one.

The rewiring is not modest, and the only question put on it came from a peer. A new Office for the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, led by the Cabinet Secretary, will house No.10, the Cabinet Office teams serving the Prime Minister directly, and a new "No.10 North" built to run devolution and local growth. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology is abolished outright: artificial-intelligence strategy and the AI Security Institute to the Cabinet Office, the digital brief to a strengthened Culture department, science and innovation to the Department for Business and Trade, renamed the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade. On the afternoon of 21 July Lord Clement-Jones put a Private Notice Question asking what that meant for the AI Safety Institute, the sovereign AI fund, the Government Digital Service and UK Research and Innovation. Baroness Anderson of Stoke-on-Trent, for the Cabinet Office, confirmed that UKRI and the sovereign fund go to the new business department, that UKRI's £9.22 billion for research in 2026-27 continues, and that the Government Digital Service goes to Culture. Asked about the shape of the ministry itself, she said what a minister in a half-made Government must: "there are ongoing appointments in government, so I cannot possibly talk about any further roles and appointments that are being made, including my own." Lord Waldegrave of North Hill, himself a former science minister, called it "the abolition and fragmentation of DSIT"; Lord Clement-Jones spoke of "the very regrettable resignation of the noble Lord, Lord Vallance," and the Minister did not demur.

What the Commons did with its last two days was heavier than its two divisions suggest. On 15 July, without a division, it approved the first use of the designation power created by the state-threats Act that reached the statute book a week earlier. The draft National Security Act 2023 (Designated Bodies) Regulations 2026 designate the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the Islamic Movement of Companions of the Right and the GRU Volunteer Corps, making it an offence punishable by up to fourteen years to support, assist or take material benefit from any of them. "This debate is a significant moment," Dame Angela Eagle told the House, "because this is the first use of a new power introduced by this Government." The Opposition supported it and complained it had taken too long. It followed a general debate on Iran in which the Minister for Veterans and People, Calvin Bailey, told Members that Iranian forces had struck two UAE tankers in the strait of Hormuz, "killing an Indian crew member and injuring eight others," and that the United Kingdom had "surged air power into the Gulf." Four Acts received Royal Assent the same afternoon, and the King sent a Message inviting the Commons to consider the provision made for the financial support of the Sovereign. The week's only divisions were deferred ones, taken on slips: a trade-union balloting order and its companion code of practice, each carried by 330 to 109.

Then, out of hours, the Government nationalised British Steel — and had to apologise for the manner of it. The Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Act had received Royal Assent on 15 July; the transfer into public ownership was executed "outside of working hours," and the regulations were not laid before Parliament before they came into force. Facing an Urgent Question on the morning of 16 July from the Liberal Democrat Joshua Reynolds, the Under-Secretary Blair McDougall opened with "regret and apologies … that we were not able to lay the regulations before Parliament before they came into force." On the final morning Steve Reed announced the largest redrawing of English local government in a generation — 38 councils in place of 134 across the areas decided, nearly 3,000 fewer councillors, and a net saving that councils themselves project at about £1 billion by 2032-33 — and Stephen Doughty reported the UK-EU treaty on Gibraltar signed in Brussels on 14 July, with the last pieces of the frontier fence gone.

The week in brief

Prime Minister's Questions, 15 July. Sir Keir Starmer took his last session as Prime Minister. The Speaker told the House from the Chair that it was the final Prime Minister's Questions with him in office. Kemi Badenoch turned to what she called this Prime Minister's final questions and paid her own tribute to Ann Widdecombe. There has been no session since, because the Commons rose the next day.

National security designations, 15 July. The Commons approved regulations designating the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the Islamic Movement of Companions of the Right and the GRU Volunteer Corps. Supporting or taking material benefit from a designated body now carries up to fourteen years in prison. There was no division.

British Steel, 15 and 16 July. The Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Act received Royal Assent on 15 July. The Government used it overnight to take British Steel into public ownership. The regulations were not laid before Parliament before they came into force. Answering an urgent question the next morning, the business minister Blair McDougall offered regret and apologies for that, and said the transfer had been done outside of working hours.

Local government reorganisation, 16 July. Steve Reed announced decisions for the remaining two-tier areas of England. Across those areas 38 councils will replace 134. He told the House there would be nearly 3,000 fewer councillors, and that councils themselves project net savings of about £1 billion by 2032-33. Elections to the new councils are due in May 2027.

The Basildon file: eighteen questions in two days

On 15 July the Housing Secretary told the House, in a written statement on Best Value, that four new Best Value Notices had been issued, and that in the case of Basildon Borough Council his concerns related to "serious cultural and governance issues," with an independent external assurance review commissioned to report by the end of October 2026. James McMurdock (Independent, South Basildon and East Thurrock) had two sitting days left. He used them to file eighteen questions on that single notice, eight on the day it was issued and ten on the last day before the rise. He asked what specific evidence led Ministers to conclude the notice was required; which Ministers and officials took the decision; on what date the Department first received the information that contributed to it; whether the review will examine procurement and contract management; what it will cost the public purse; and whether its findings will be published in full. It is the written question used as a preservation order: not to change the outcome, which is already announced, but to fix the sequence of events in the record before six weeks of recess close over it.

From the written answers

Schools: Temperature. Damian Hinds (Conservative, East Hampshire) asked the Education Secretary what steps her Department is taking to ensure that schools remain safe and operational during periods of extreme heat. The answer: the department takes periods of hot weather and heatwaves seriously and has published a climate risk assessment. In most cases, schools are the safest place for children during hot weather, as staff can actively monitor pupils, ensure regular hydration, and make adjustments to keep them cool. Children may also not be any cooler at home than in school.
