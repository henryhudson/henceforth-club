Design spec · 2026-07-09 · xtext · scope: D of four · app-side (FORTHapp)

Archiving someone pays them

The reward you already pay to archive a profile goes to the person you archived, if they have said where to send it — so preserving someone becomes a way to pay them, and claiming your account becomes worth doing.

What exists. Spec A shipped the showroom; spec C shipped the handle-to-key binding, so an account can commit an identity address on X (Verifying my Henceforth identity: <address>), prove control by signing, and wear a verified tick. Every xtext archive already pays a reward — twice the miner fee, capped at 50,000 satoshis — to a single fixed developer address (xArchiveRewardAddress, 1mSxVgV1h7crdb8Qr3e7o3H18GczcUsiC). The archive transaction is built entirely in the Henceforth app; the website is not involved in paying it.

The one-line thesis: point that reward at the address the archived account committed to, instead of the developer. The account already published where to send its funds; D simply sends them there.

Why it closes the sequence. Spec C’s whole promise was that claiming your account is worth doing. D is the reason. Once you have bound an address, anyone who archives you — a reader preserving a voice they value — pays you, not us. And archiving yourself returns the reward to your own address, so it costs only the miner fee. The bounty is not a new pool of money; it is the reward that already changes hands, redirected to the person it is about.

1 · Where it lives

App-side only. The website needs no change. Spec C put the owner’s address on X and on chain and taught the site to read it. D reads the same address and chooses it as the reward output’s destination in the transaction the app already builds. There is no new endpoint, no site query, no server code — one output address differs, decided inside broadcastXArchive.

2 · The mechanic

Before building the archive transaction, parse the fetched timeline (pending.fullArchive.posts) for the exact binding line spec C defined — Verifying my Henceforth identity: <address> — and read the committed address out of it. Then choose the reward output’s destination: 
owner address found in the archived timeline  →  reward pays the owner
none                                          →  reward pays the developer (unchanged)
Everything else is identical: the same reward amount, the same single output, the same non-custodial build. One address is different.

The address is read from the full fetched timeline, not only the newly-inscribed delta, because the binding tweet may have been inscribed in an earlier archive and is no longer among the new posts. C checks the inscribed posts (the site must see the commitment at that transaction to grant the tick); D checks the whole timeline (it only needs to know where the account said to send funds). Same line, read for a different purpose.

Claimed users stop paying the developer the reward on their own archives. This is a deliberate, accepted change. The reward is about £0.006; the developer’s income is the app sale, not this. Redirecting the reward to owners — including the self-archive, where it returns to you — is the whole point of D, and the near-zero sum makes the trade costless in practice.

4 · Trust and safety

The routed address is trustless by construction. Only the account can post to its own timeline, so the only binding address the app can find in @jack’s real timeline is @jack’s own. Someone who fabricates a fake @jack archive containing their own address routes the reward to themselves — they pay their own address, and there is no victim. The site’s verified-tick check (which requires a signature) is unaffected; a fabrication earns no tick.
  • Route on the commitment, not on the site record. D reads the address from the archived timeline, not from the site’s x:owner record, so it needs no site call and inherits no site-trust for the payout. The rare difference — an account that published its address but never completed the signed claim — still receives routing, which is correct: it stated its payout. The signature is what grants the tick, not what directs the money.
  • Non-custodial, no escrow, no retroactive routing. The destination is chosen at build time from data already in hand. Nothing is held; nothing accrues. An owner who claims after being archived does not retroactively collect — only future archives pay them. This is the direct consequence of the no-escrow constraint, stated plainly rather than worked around.
  • The residual trust already exists. The app trusts the fetched timeline to build the archive at all; D adds no new trusted input. And the sum at stake per archive is about £0.006.

5 · The shared address

C and D read the same value: the identity address the account committed to. C uses it as the thing a signature must prove control of, to grant the verified tick. D uses it as the thing the reward pays. One address, published once, does both jobs — the ownership proof and the payout destination are the same string, which is exactly what made C worth building before D.

6 · Definition of done

  • A pure function — rewardPayeeAddress(archivedPosts, developerAddress) → address — that returns the binding address when the timeline carries one, and the developer address otherwise. No wallet, no network; the three cases above become assertions, plus a fourth: a fabricated binding line containing a malformed address falls through to the developer rather than producing an invalid payee.
  • A test that the reward amount is unchanged — D moves the destination, never the sum — so a regression that also changed the amount is caught.
  • The parser shares the exact prefix constant with the claim path (spec C), so the two can never drift on what a binding line looks like.
  • Not assertable, and said out loud: whether anyone actually archives anyone else. With one bound profile today, D is mostly mechanism; its economy waits on a corpus. The code is correct and ready; the flywheel is a launch question, not a test.

7 · Scope — deliberately one output address

In: the reward output’s destination, chosen from the binding tweet. That is the whole feature.

Out: a bounty board (someone posts a bounty to get a profile archived), a pool, a reimbursement waterfall, and any “owner earnings” dashboard on the site. The showroom spec called the marketplace flywheel empty until there is a corpus, and D does not need it: routing the reward that already moves is complete on its own, and unlocks the marketplace later without presupposing it now.

8 · Open questions

  • Surfacing the payout to the archiver. When the reward routes to an owner, the terminal could say so — “your reward went to @jack, the verified owner” — so the archiver understands where their money went and why. A one-line addition; the exact wording is a plan-level detail.
  • Multiple binding lines. If a timeline somehow carries more than one binding line (an account that rotated keys), the parser must pick one deterministically — the most recent by post order is the natural rule, matching “latest binding wins” from spec C’s rotation note. The plan pins this.
  • Legal, deferred to the same lawyer as A and C. Routing a payment to a person identified by their social handle is a light touch, but it is a payment to a third party; worth a glance alongside the archival-permanence questions already raised.
