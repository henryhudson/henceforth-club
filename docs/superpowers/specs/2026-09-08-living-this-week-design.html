A sheet that changes, not an article that grows
The card was written in August, when This Week in Parliament was an article. Since 1.10 it is a printed sheet the application fetches as a portable document. That one change settles most of the design, and withdraws the loosening the card asked for.

The seam is not new. It already runs down the middle of the pipeline

The editorial specification already forbids the model to write two fields. Step six says do not write mostActive, the wrapper computes and injects it; and do not write money either, because every figure there is quoted from a minister's own answer, and that is what lets the sheet keep claiming at the foot of the page that every figure was checked against the record. The wrapper then does exactly that, after the model has stopped.

So the pipeline already holds two kinds of content apart, and already trusts a plain script with one of them. The living week is not a new idea about the digest. It is the same seam, drawn further along: everything a script can derive from Parliament's own record moves to the daily side, and everything that needs a person stays on Wednesday.

The lead is the case that proves the rule. The specification ranks candidates by the prominence of the proceeding, the volume of contributions, a surge of questions on one subject, outside salience with a real parliamentary hook, and gravity, then tells the editor plainly that the busiest is not the biggest. None of that can be settled on a Monday. Writing the lead daily would not build it; it would discard and rewrite it four times to arrive at the paragraph Wednesday writes anyway.

Two · the shape · one sheet, rewritten daily, with an editorial top that lands on Wednesday

Rewritten, not appended. Each run recomputes the mechanical fields over the whole window and writes the file again. Appending would be wrong within a fortnight: a division amended, a question re-tabled, a bill stage corrected, and the appended row is stale for good. Recomputing a window is idempotent. Appending to one is not.

A spine of dated entries is the wrong artefact. The sheet is four packed columns ordered by fit, and the divisions square is deliberately sorted closest first, not by day. A day spine would cost that ordering and buy very little, for the reason boxed alongside.

What a reader sees on a Tuesday. A complete looking page, set larger. The fit loop starts at seven points and climbs to ten when the columns do not fill the sheet, so a Tuesday page carrying six divisions instead of twenty is set at nine or ten points and reads as a generous register page rather than a thin one. Wednesday's finished sheet comes back down to seven or below. That is a property of the shipped renderer, not a hope.

The limit worth saying out loud

A portable document has no memory of who opened it last, so it cannot mark what is new since a reader's last visit, and the divisions are not even ordered so the newest sit at one end. All a sheet can carry is a line saying how far the week has run. Sell it as freshness, not as a page a reader watches.

The print budget

The sheet refuses rather than clips: the packer records the overflow, and the render reads it, fails, and will not open the result, because a clipped sheet is not a proof.

The type band is six points to ten and growth is one directional, so the shedding order is declared in advance and never chosen at render time.

  1. The divisions square caps and prints its own "and so many more" row.
  2. The subjects cap falls from ten to eight.
  3. The department chart cap falls from eight to six.
  4. The questions and answers square drops.

All three caps are constants in the component today. Making them a function of the measured overflow is the whole change. If the page still will not hold at six points, the run leaves yesterday's sheet standing and says so.

Three · the publishing rules · a daily build and one human flip, living together without touching each other

The flip stays Henry's alone and nothing about it changes. The provisional sheet carries no editorial prose whatsoever: no lead, no feature, no questions and answers, only counts, divisions, money and registers, every figure quoted from the record. There is nothing half written on it to leak, which is a cleaner loosening than the card proposed. The card asked for the building draft to render publicly on the web page; the code goes out of its way to prevent that, gating even the page's card metadata on published status so a draft's headline and lede are never republished at a public address. Keep the article gated and loosen the sheet only.

Two consequences that are real work. The index the application reads serves only published summaries, so a building week that is not listed is invisible whatever sheet exists; the route must list published and building, while the archive and the week page keep the filter they have. And the committed gate asserts today that a draft has no sheet; it must become: a published week must have a sheet, a building week may have one, and any sheet belonging to a week that is not published must come from a digest carrying none of the editorial fields. That last clause makes "no unfinished prose is printed" a test rather than a promise.

A plain script every morning, and a person every Wednesday
The daily half needs no model and no change to the application. Its safety is one sentence: it is a monotone writer confined to a single file the editorial pass has not yet claimed.
Four · the machinery · what runs, when, where, and what it must never write

What runs. One script in Node, with no model in it at all. It fetches the sources the pipeline already names: the Commons votes search and its separate total, the written questions paged a hundred at a time, the bills list by last update, the non sitting calendar, and the members snapshot that diffs the whole House. It recomputes the mechanical fields over the whole window, writes the file at status building, and calls the existing render with a provisional flag.

When, which is the card's open question. Inside the morning routine, not on a new scheduled job. The evidence is plain: the scheduled job written for the Wednesday digest on 2 July has never been installed on any machine. It is absent from the user launch agents here, absent from the loaded job list, and its log file has never been created. A schedule that has not fired once in nine weeks is not the place to hang a job that must fire five times a week. The morning routine does run daily and is watched, so a failure is seen the morning it happens rather than written to a temporary file nobody opens. The machine that would host a new job is also the undersized mini already carrying three runners.

A morning run also reads better data than the evening run the August sketch proposed. Parliament's record for a sitting day is not settled while the day is still running, which is why the application's own feed groups on complete days in the record's zone. A morning build always reads yesterday whole.

Where. The site checkout, the same one the weekly wrapper uses and pulls before it starts.

What it writes, and what it must never write

Writes. The coming Wednesday's digest file at status building, and that week's sheet.

Never writes. The headline, the lede, the body, the feature, the questions and answers, the overview headline, intro, feature or brief, and no blurb on any vote or bill. It never sets the status to draft or published.

Never touches. Any file whose status is not building or absent. Once the editorial pass has claimed the week the machine is finished with it. Those two refusals are the whole safety argument, and both are assertable without a network.

How it fails safely

A source that fails leaves its own field as the file had it, logged and never silenced. That is the rule the application's own fortnight feed follows when one of its five sources does not answer, and it should be copied rather than reasoned about again.

A render that overflows leaves yesterday's sheet at the address; the published sheet is only replaced by one that fitted. A run that fails entirely leaves both file and sheet as they were.

Order does not matter on a Wednesday. A run before the editorial pass finds the file building and updates it. A run after finds it at draft and stops.

Five · what it costs and what it earns

Cost. A day for the first step and the third state, plus a standing step in the morning routine. One route change, one gate change, one shed ladder made dynamic. Nothing in the application.

For the reader, almost nothing this month. The Hansard drew 546 impressions, 36 product page views and two downloads in the week. Two people. Fresher copy on a Tuesday changes what a very small number of existing readers see one day earlier. It does not change how many arrive, and the reach plan's own answer for this app is the September play and the weekly post, not the freshness of the sheet.

For the writer, a real return. Wednesday's pass re-derives the week from cold inside one model run: two thousand written questions paged in hundreds, six hundred and fifty members diffed, the record searched for the lead, before a word is written. With the counts, the histogram, the money and every division row already in the file, that budget goes wholly to ranking the week and writing the lead.

So be honest. This is a production improvement wearing the clothes of a reader feature. Build it for the Wednesday, and count the reader gain at nil until there are readers to gain.

Six · the smallest first step, and it fits inside a normal Wednesday

One run, not five. On a Monday, run the mechanical build once by hand over the week so far, render it with a provisional flag, and publish it at the coming Wednesday's address. Then open the application on a device holding the 19 August sheet and watch it follow.

That single run exercises everything the design turns on: the third state, the index listing a building week, the amended gate, a sheet carrying no editorial prose, and supersession at the same address when Wednesday's finished sheet lands over it. It fits a day because the render already exists and the change is a flag and a status.

Its precondition is already carded. Making the flip render and publish the sheet in the same act was named on the sheet card on 2 September and has not been done. Without it the finished sheet still has to be rendered by hand, and the living week would end its week worse than it began: a provisional page standing at the address while the finished one waits on a manual command.

What not to build first. Not the five day schedule. Not a day spine on the divisions. Not any change to the application, which already does the following, the landing and the once a session refresh that this design depends on.

How you know it worked. A device that opened This Week before the run, and holds the 19 August sheet, opens it after the run and is standing on the current week without being told to move. If it does not move, the fault is in the index rather than the sheet, because the walk only considers weeks the index lists.

The one decision left for Henry. Whether a provisional sheet may carry the nameplate at all. It will look like an edition of The Hansard, because it is set from the same components, and it will say in its own masthead that the week is unfinished. If that is too close to an edition, the alternative is to hold the provisional sheet back and accept that the current week stays invisible until the flip, which is exactly today's behaviour and today's three week lag.

What the reading contradicted in the card
  1. The public building page is neither needed nor wanted. The card asks for the building draft to render publicly on the site, and calls it a deliberate loosening of the draft gate. The application does not read that page; it fetches a portable document. Loosen the sheet and leave the article gated, which is both smaller and safer.
  2. A reader cannot watch it fill. A printed sheet has no memory of its reader and the divisions are ordered by closeness, not by day. The most the artefact can carry is a stated line saying how far the week has run.
  3. There is no running schedule to extend. The card frames the daily trigger as a choice between the scheduled job on the mini and the morning routine. The scheduled job has never been installed anywhere, so the morning routine wins on evidence rather than on preference.
  4. The evening is the wrong hour. The sketch proposed a sitting day evening run, which races the record's own settling. A morning run reads complete days, which is the rule the application already keeps.
  5. Freshness is not the complaint. The card assumes the week is written late. It is written on time and published late. On 8 September the newest published sheet is 19 August, because two finished digests are waiting on a flip. A daily build would have written five more sheets into the same closed gate.
